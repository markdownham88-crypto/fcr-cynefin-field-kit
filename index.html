01 · The field

Bounded applicability in the receiver’s field

Cynefin is a Welsh word for the many intertwined factors that shape how we think, interpret and act. The framework built on it is, at heart, a decision-support framework resting on bounded applicability: there are few, if any, context-free solutions but many valid context-specific ones. Its main use is to help choose an approach and to recognise when that approach should change.

That is the receiver’s problem exactly. An appointment arrives with a standard instruction, a context-free solution: secure, market, sell, account. Sometimes the context fits it. Often it does not, and it rarely fits all of the appointment at once. Notice of appointment and day-one insurance are routine and should be done the same way every time. Title and valuation need expertise. Tenants, the street and the buyer pool act on one another in ways that make sense only in hindsight. A fire in a vacant unit demands action before analysis. And sometimes the honest answer to “what kind of problem is this?” is “we do not yet know”.

The first act of a receiver is not to sell but to ask what kind of system this is.

The earlier treatises in this series asked that question through Boyd’s orientation, through design thinking, through complex responsive processes and through the practice of reframing. This one asks it through Cynefin, and specifically through the v45º revision of 2013: the moment when Snowden rotated the four domain models, stepped them, and joined their lines of coherence into one path. That path is the shape of a well-run receivership.

03 · Cynefin v45º

One sheet, four models

On 8 September 2013, on a rest day in Tuscany, Snowden wrote that he had settled down to complete the v45º domain models. Three changes had come out of an advanced seminar in Seattle the month before: wording and label changes on most of the models; rotating them 45º to make the coherence path clearer; and stepping them so that one overlaps with the other. The hard part, he wrote, was getting all four domain models onto a single sheet with the coherence and movement lines in the right place. The step-shift idea he credited to Rob England.

The logic of the sheet is energetic. Complex, complicated and simple form a stepped progression, because more energy is required to move to a more ordered domain. Chaos steps down to complex, because chaos is hard to sustain: either you collapse, or constraint comes into play and you are dealing with the complex. And simple, the most ordered, collapses catastrophically into chaos. Snowden imagined two further forms: a three-dimensional version with hollows in each domain and a central slippery slope to a hole representing disorder; and the combined image printed and wrapped around an asymmetric cylinder, so that the end of the path meets its beginning.

The sheet below is drawn afresh from those descriptions. Each diamond is a domain model rotated 45º, so its line of coherence runs horizontally and joins the next. Tap any cell for its meaning and its receivership scenario. Switch to the slope view for the three-dimensional idea, or play the path.

04 · The grammar of a domain model

Three by three, a line, two corners

Every domain model shares a grammar. It is a three-by-three matrix on two dimensions. The diagonal from bottom left to top right is the line of coherence, a sensible pathway of transition, in homage to Ashby and Boisot. On the line, things are OK; slip off it and you have problems and must return. The corners off the line mark potential transitions to disorder. Bottom left leans toward a less ordered domain, top right toward a more ordered one. Colour carries meaning: red for deviations needing urgent attention, blue for management action, green for deviations worth using.

Snowden insists the models are “a cook’s handbook, not a recipe”: guidance and reminders, deliberately semi-developed, whose labels organisations should rename in their own language. Here they are renamed, cell by cell, in the receiver’s. Each of the next four sections holds one model. Each has a Rotate 45º control that turns the matrix so you can watch the diagonal become the horizontal path of the v45º sheet, and a Run all nine control that plays every cell’s scenario in turn.

05 · Clear

The Clear domain model: routine and its cliff

Snowden called the Simple domain “boring and unsexy for a reason”, and insisted that no domain is bad in itself. In v45º its labels moved: “evidence based” became “self-evident”, and the domain that once sat bottom-left now sits at the extreme right of the sheet with chaos one step below. Its dimensions are the attitude of actors (complacent, pragmatic, questioning), which can be influenced but not controlled, and compliance enforcement (zombie-like, trade-off, self-evident), which management controls.

The dynamic is a life cycle. Practice enters as a trade-off, standardisation accepted as the price of consistency. It becomes self-evidently right and the sensible response is pragmatic acceptance: best practice, where movement should stop. The danger is the constant trade-off between standardisation and ossification. When practice is taken for truth without question, enforcement turns zombie-like; cynics give early warning; informal networks start gaming; and traumatic failure follows. In a receivership, Clear is where the day-one routine lives, and where the uninsured fire comes from.

Drift towards the fold

The Clear model’s most important feature is the edge it sits beside. Order that is over-constrained, or that people have stopped noticing, does not decay gently into something more complex. It holds, apparently unchanged, until it falls into chaos all at once. That is the catastrophic fold. Its mechanism is complacency: workarounds tolerated because nothing went wrong last time, audits that check the paperwork rather than the stair, weak signals reported by nobody because nobody asked.

A receivership in its routine phase is exactly this kind of system. Rent comes in, the agent closes tickets, the monthly report is reassuring. The instrument below runs thirty-six months of such an appointment. Thirteen small failures arrive on a timeline: a wedged fire door, a late gas certificate, an unlocked roof hatch. Whether each one is caught depends on how much the appointment invests in weak-signal reporting. Drift accumulates with tolerated workarounds and is reduced by audit. Try the presets, then set the sliders yourself. The point to notice is not that neglect fails, which is obvious, but how normal the ledge looks right up to the edge, and how long the way back is once the ball has gone over.

06 · Complicated

The Complicated domain model: strategy and engagement

The v45º Complicated model is a three-by-three rotated so that the coherence pathway becomes a horizontal line “with a twist”, drawn with a gradient because more energy is needed to move toward order. Its dimensions are strategic focus (forming the strategy, closing down options, full exploitation) and engagement (a few key players, building consensus, mandated consent).

Along the line run three stages. Exploration to exploitation, in a small group, with safe-to-fail experiments as the criterion for moving on. Closing down and wider engagement, ideally before others lose their chance to shape the direction. Universal buy-in, where behaviour is constrained to process and performance measured. Two extremes slip into disorder. At the top, full exploitation without wider engagement produces compliance and disguised activity. At the bottom, premature engagement lets entrenched practice defeat the better idea: Snowden’s Longitude problem. This is the domain of the receivership strategy report.

Triangulating experts, and ritual dissent

Complicated is the domain of experts, and its characteristic failure is that the experts agree too easily, or on the wrong thing. Snowden and Boone’s danger signals for complicated contexts include experts overconfident in their own solutions and the views of outsiders being dismissed; in receivership the most common form is anchoring. The first figure spoken in the room is usually the loan balance, and every subsequent opinion of value bends towards it. The result looks like consensus and reads as Clear. It is neither.

The instrument sets five experts on one mixed-use block, each on a different basis: market value subject to tenancies, a break-up sale of the flats, an auction lot, value after a remedial allowance, and an investment yield. Their ranges overlap only partly, and the honest reading is Complicated: settle the basis, then the figure. Switch to “Anchored on the loan” and watch the ranges close on £2.40m and the diagnosis flip, falsely, to Clear.

Then run ritual dissent. In Snowden’s method a spokesperson presents a proposal and turns their back while the group attacks it (dissent) or offers positive alternatives (assent); the ritual makes it a game, less personal, and forces listening rather than defending. Each challenge here removes an assumption the figures were quietly resting on: the missing EWS1 form, the sublet flats, the lapsed planning consent. Two assents add routes the experts had not priced. By the third challenge the spread has widened past a third of the median and the question has moved into the Complex: the figure is not knowable by analysis alone, and the market must be probed.

07 · Complex

The Complex domain model: consensus and coherence

The Complex model was the most stable of the four; Snowden said it remained so bar the 45º reorientation. Its April 2013 edition replaced earlier circles with nine distinct areas and marked the diagonal explicitly. Its dimensions are the degree of consensus (the cognoscenti, orthodoxy, group think) and the level of coherence of the evidence (abductive, inductive, beyond reasonable doubt).

The natural dynamic runs along the diagonal: a new idea starts with a few believers and weak evidence, and as evidence builds and consensus spreads it moves from exploration toward exploitation. The bottom-left corner is exaptation, the edge of chaos, with the most creativity and opportunity; most people start instead in the centre with a token probe. Top-left is group think: high consensus without evidence and a high risk of sudden collapse, to be broken up. Bottom-right are the heretics and mavericks, to be used: give them a sandpit. In a receivership, Complex is where the tenants, the street and the buyer pool live.

A portfolio of safe-to-fail probes

The defining practice of the Complex domain is the parallel, safe-to-fail probe. Snowden’s 2007 formulation is a decision rule set before opinions harden: everyone with an idea that has even a remote chance of being useful designs a small experiment around it, and every experiment must be observable so that success can be amplified and failure dampened. The Cynefin wiki adds the portfolio tests: probes built on coherent hypotheses, small, local and recoverable, deliberately varied and contradictory, some oblique, some naïve, each with its own budget and time-frame. Its sharpest rule is the one receivers most need to hear: if some probes do not fail, you are playing it too safe.

The instrument applies this to a half-let parade of eight shops with flats above. Fourteen candidate probes sit under four rival hypotheses: it is the rents, the place, the mix, or the sale route. Two candidates are traps. Waiting for the market is not a probe, because nothing about it can be observed. A full refurbishment is coherent but not safe-to-fail. Choose up to eight, check the portfolio against the eight tests, and run twelve weeks. At weeks four and eight, amplify the lanes showing promise and dampen the rest, or let the automatic rule do it. Each probe has a hidden propensity, so the parade answers differently from the prediction. “New world” shifts those propensities: the same portfolio in a different parade. The lesson the simulation keeps teaching is that the pattern is found, not chosen, and that the contradictory pairs (hold rents against turnover rents, seal the empties against a meanwhile use) carry most of the information.

08 · Chaotic

The Chaotic domain model: crisis, innovation, death and rebirth

The earlier Chaotic model had two dimensions: the ability to impose constraints (impossible, unknown, easy) and awareness of the shift into chaos (unexpected, plausible, deliberate). Only the top right is valid: deliberate entry where constraints are easy, the zone of managed innovation. The bottom left carried the medieval cartographer’s warning, here be dragons. In v45º the vertical labels became known, unknown and unknowable, with the need to impose constraint rising as the unknowable approaches, until there is no point. The model gained a fuller account of death and rebirth, with two exits: early recognition and the creation of something new, or hanging on into inauthentic disorder with no return. It also showed three types of collapse from Simple.

For receivers this is the domain of fire, flood, squatters, building-safety failures and safeguarding: act first, then sense. It is also, deliberately entered, a source of innovation, and Snowden’s advice that an innovation team should run alongside every crisis team is one of the most useful sentences a receiver can borrow.

The first hours: a containment drill

In the Chaotic domain there is no time to work out the relationship between cause and effect, because the relationships are being made and unmade by the event. The response is act, sense, respond: impose a constraint, any workable constraint, to stop the spread; then look at what the constraint revealed; then respond. The practice of the domain is novel practice: the first act is often a constraint that did not exist a minute ago. Its characteristic mistake is to wait for full information.

The drill makes that concrete for a receiver woken at two in the morning. A fault in the plant room of a block of ten flats above two shops is spreading along the paths a building actually has: the stair core, the lift shaft, the ceiling voids, the bin store next door. Each hour you have two actions. Secure a space to stop the spread through it, or fight a space that is already burning. Then compare “Act first” with “Wait for full information”, which holds every action until hour three. The comparison is not subtle, and that is the point: in chaos the value of an action decays by the hour. When the drill ends, the instrument does the sensing for you. The stair core and lift shaft, which connect most of the building, are ringed as the pattern to carry into the rebuild as permanent constraints: compartmentation, fire-stopping, an evacuation strategy that does not depend on one stair.

09 · The framework now

Five domains, four liminal states, one fold

Cynefin has moved on since 2013. At its heart are three primary domains defined by the type of constraint or its absence: order, complexity and chaos. Order divides into Clear (cause and effect self-evident; rigid or fixed constraints) and Complicated (cause and effect discoverable by expertise; governing constraints). Complex has enabling constraints and many levels of entanglement: it is dispositional, with no linear material causality. Chaos is the absence of effective constraint. At the centre is Confusion, whose liminal part has since March 2020 been called the aporetic: confusion held knowingly. Disorder, which once covered both authentic and inauthentic states, was split into aporetic and confused. Simple became Obvious and then Clear.

A liminal line, open at the top and closed at the bottom, runs through every domain except Clear. The boundary between Clear and Chaos is a catastrophic fold: a cliff, where the liminality in Clear is invisible and it is easy to walk off through excessive confidence in rigid constraints. 10 · The ages of Cynefin

From a napkin to a framework of passages

The Cynefin wiki tells the framework’s story in ages. It emerged at the close of the 1990s from disquiet with knowledge management in large corporations: Snowden was challenging Nonaka and Takeuchi’s model of converting tacit to explicit knowledge, and Max Boisot’s I-Space supplied the attention to abstraction and codification and to the difference between contexts where cause and effect can be predicted and those where they cannot. The first reference was a 1999 CBI publication; in 2000 a “back of a napkin” drawing already distinguished five domains: two ordered, one complex, one chaotic and one confused. A second age brought wavy cross lines, the central domain and the 2003 dynamics; a third, from 2007, brought the Harvard Business Review article with Mary Boone and the weak-signal work that became SenseMaker. Liminality, the aporetic turn and the renaming of Obvious as Clear followed.

The name, in the wiki’s gloss, means the “place of your multiple belongings”. The wiki is emphatic about what the framework is not: not a model that predicts structure, not a quadrant diagram on Cartesian axes, not a method. It is a way of deciding which method to use and when to change it. Step through the timeline; each moment carries its lesson for receivers.

The principles, in the receiver’s hand

The wiki now gathers Cynefin’s heuristics under three overarching principles: embrace messy coherence; descriptive self-awareness and self-discovery; and timing and flow. Turn the cards for each heuristic and its receivership reading.

11 · The iterations

Every published iteration, redrawn and credited

Cynefin has been drawn many times since 2000, and each drawing records a change in thinking. The napkin of 2000 already had five domains. The 2003 IBM Systems Journal version named them known, knowable, complex and chaos around a central disorder, and drew wavy rather than straight boundaries. The 2007 Harvard Business Review exhibit renamed the ordered pair simple and complicated, attached the practices (best, good, emergent, novel) and the decision models, and placed simple next to chaotic “for good reason”. In 2013 each domain gained its own three-by-three model. Simple became Obvious in 2014. A liminal line arrived in 2017. Between 2019 and 2021 disorder split into aporetic and confused, Obvious became Clear, and the domains came to be defined by constraint. In 2026 the Cynefin Company’s machines-and-humans lens drew the classic framework again, this time to decide what should be automated.

The originals belong to their authors and publishers, and this page does not reproduce them. Each iteration below is redrawn from its published description, and the panel carries its credit. Step through them and watch the framework morph, switch the layers, or compare any two side by side.

12 · A leader’s framework

The 2007 leader’s table, translated for receivers

Snowden and Boone’s 2007 article made Cynefin famous in management with one table: for each context, what it is like, what the leader’s job is, what danger signals to watch for, and how to respond when they appear. Its central claim is that leaders must recognise which context governs a situation and change their style to fit, and that the most frequent collapses into chaos happen because success has bred complacency. Their richest example was the deputy police chief in Palatine, Illinois, in 1993, who in one case of multiple murders worked in all four contexts at once. He kept routine policing running, brought in experts, kept the community informed in the chaotic hours, and then created a forum where residents could hear the facts and air their fears.

13 · Machines and humans

A Cynefin lens on the receiver’s work: what to automate, what must stay human

In February 2026 the Cynefin Company published Mahathir Rosul’s “A Cynefin Framework Lens: Where Machines Work Best and Why Humans Remain Indispensable”, written with Rhiannon Davies. It declines to argue about what machines can do in general. It starts instead from the nature of the situation, and only then decides who or what should act. Its diagram is the classic framework: four domains (Clear, Complicated, Complex, Chaotic) around a central zone of aporia and confusion. Each domain sets the roles of machines and people.

In Clear, machines dominate by design: they automate, run checklists and rules engines and manage workflows, with speed and consistency people cannot match in repetitive work. People design the rules, monitor for drift and intervene when conditions change. In Complicated, machines process data, surface correlations and support optimisation, but they cannot understand context, trade-offs or consequences: they assist, and people decide among good practices. In Complex, machines can detect patterns after probes have run, amplify feedback and support learning, but they cannot create meaning; they know that something happened, not why it matters. People must lead the probing. In Chaotic, leaders act first, create order and only then sense: “a fundamentally human responsibility”. In the centre, confusion is a state to escape, and aporia is deliberate, generative uncertainty: the pause before clarity, where reframing happens. Here, the post argues, people are foundational, because someone must decide which domain applies before any capability is used.

For receivership the lens is immediately practical, and it has a legal edge. Software can generate rent demands; it cannot hold a duty of good faith. Analytics can find comparables; they cannot take reasonable care to obtain true market value. Sensors can detect a leak; they cannot command an evacuation. 14 · Constraints in depth

Change the constraints and you change the domain

Cynefin now defines its domains by constraint: rigid or fixed in Clear, governing in Complicated, enabling in Complex, and none effective in Chaos. Snowden found that pairing (fixed or governing for order, enabling for complexity) too coarse, and set out to build a fuller typology from two further distinctions. The first is containers and connectors: constraints that hold things in, and constraints that link things together. The second is robust and resilient. A robust system survives unchanged or with minor modification, with explicit boundaries: “shoring it up until Christmas”. A resilient system keeps its identity by changing, sometimes in ways that are hard to see.

From that typology comes a method of constraint mapping, which starts indirectly to avoid premature convergence. Introduce the typology. Identify the constraints at different levels. Map how controllable each one is. Separate those we can change from those only others can change. Assess the risk of each change. Pick the low-risk changes. Then run parallel safe-to-fail experiments on them. For a receiver this is the most practical idea in the whole framework. The appointment’s domain is not fixed. It moves when its constraints move, and some of those constraints are the receiver’s to change.

15 · Estuarine mapping

The estuary: a map of what can change

Constraint mapping grew into the third major framework of the Cynefin ecosystem: the estuarine framework, an energy and time grid with the actants of a situation placed on it. The metaphor is an estuary, not a delta: many shifting flows, some features that barely move from one year to the next, others that change with every tide, and some things that can only be done at the turn. For a receiver it may be the single most practical framework in the whole family, because it answers the question that every appointment asks in its first week: of everything here, what can I actually change, how fast, and at what cost?

Three kinds of thing are mapped. Constraints shape a system’s patterns and possibilities, and are not mainly barriers: they connect or they contain. Constructors, a term borrowed from Deutsch and Marletto’s constructor theory, produce consistent, repeatable transformations; a managing agent’s monthly routine, which turns arrears into chasers and defects into tickets, is a constructor. Actors act with intelligence and intention: individuals, roles, collectives. Everything is placed by the energy and time it would take to change it, whatever anyone wants. Mapping records what is, not what is hoped for.

Then come the borders. A counterfactual border cuts off the top right. Above it, for now, nothing changes, and the group must work with what is there; items can be negotiated across it or broken into parts. Inside it the optional liminal line marks things that can change, but not by those doing the mapping: in a receivership, the credit committee, the council, the tribunal’s timetable. Round the bottom left the volatile line marks things that change quickly and cheaply. In other frameworks that would be low-hanging fruit. Here it is a warning: if something important can change that easily, it can change against you too, and the first move is to raise the cost of changing it. The tenants’ WhatsApp group and the local agents’ gossip are the obvious cases; both can take a sale price down in a week. What remains between the borders is the action zone.

What is done with the map is specific. Vector actions change things: the compass rose alters the cost of changing an item by changing the space around it rather than the item itself, and there are also destroy and stabilise. Signal actions watch and wait: monitor (deep in counterfactual space a monitor becomes a forward scout), trigger, and conditional. Communication actions reach out: research, request, and interaction, which creates, severs or conditions connections. The actions become micro-projects, and the micro-projects are combined into portfolios. Behind all of it sits the Vector Theory of Change: start from where you are with a sense of direction rather than an abstract goal; work with propensities so that what you want costs less energy than what you do not; prefer many small nudges to one large project; decompose to the lowest coherent level of granularity; and know where you can monitor, amplify and dampen.

The instrument maps nineteen actants of a parade appointment. Select one to read it and act on it. Drag it to argue about its placement; on the wiki, disagreement about placement is a signal to decompose. Drag the orange and blue handles to move the counterfactual and volatile borders, and watch actants change zone. Or run the eight-step method, from pre-process to portfolio, and let the instrument propose a first portfolio of micro-projects.

      16 · Phase shifts

      Latent heat and the triple point

      Shifts between the primary domains are phase shifts. Snowden’s metaphor is latent heat: energy is needed to turn liquid into gas although the temperature stays the same, and turning liquid into solid gives energy off. The v45º sheet drew the same truth as steps. The centre of the framework is a triple point, where a system is equally likely to move into any of the three states; triple points calibrate thermometers, and the first move out of a crisis, from chaos into the aporetic, is a rapid calibration.

      For a receiver the lesson is about effort and timing. Imposing governing constraints on an appointment (a strategy, a process, a mandate) costs energy and the effect is slow to show, like heat going into melting ice. Collapse releases energy all at once. Drag the slider to add or remove constraint, and watch where the plateaus fall.

      17 · Dynamics

      Lines, steps and movements

      Cynefin is about movement as much as position. The wiki now describes three coloured dynamics and four inflection points. The blue line progresses to stability: from many safe-to-fail probes in Complex, through the liminal space where repeatability is confirmed, into Complicated, and into Clear only if stable, a costly and hard-to-reverse commitment. The red line is the route of radical change when stability is false, sustained by constraints people have become immune to and by workarounds: through aporia, optionally a shallow dive into chaos, and back into Complex. The purple line is grazing: a near-continuous cycle where patterns never settle. Four points mark the decisions: alpha (repeatability before Complicated), beta (does Complicated still hold?), gamma (suspension in aporia: decompose, recompose, exapt) and delta (the last check before Clear).

      18 · The aporetic turn

      Knowing that you do not know

      Aporia is Greek for an impasse, a difficulty in making passage. In Cynefin, drawing on Derrida and first suggested by John van Breda, it names a paradox or unanswerable uncertainty held knowingly. The aporetic turn, added in 2019 and 2020, is the legitimate, deliberate transition out of the central domain into another. Snowden increasingly treats the aporetic as the starting point for sense-making: assume you are in the centre, make the confusion as aporetic as possible, and reduce the granularity of the problem until aspects become exclusively complex or ordered. Being unknowingly confused is the danger, and the confused domain sits next to the catastrophic fold for a reason.

      Most receiverships begin here, whether or not anyone says so. The appointment is several domains at once, and the duties pull in different directions. Choose a move to see it worked on an appointment.

      19 · The children’s party

      Managing the complex parade like a children’s party

      Snowden’s best-known parable contrasts three ways of running a party for eleven-year-old boys. Treat it as chaos and the result is harmful and unrecoverable. Treat it as order (learning objectives, a project plan, milestones, pocket money tied to targets) and it is rigid and detached from the people at the party. The complex way is the one every parent already knows: set flexible boundaries, introduce catalytic probes, notice the attractors that form, amplify the good and dampen the bad, monitor, and have the “bad party” responses planned in advance. You know whether it was a good party only at the end.

      A tenanted parade in receivership is a children’s party. Step through the six moves on the plan of the parade.

      20 · Crisis

      Assess, adapt, exapt, transcend

      In 2020 Snowden and Alessandro Rancati wrote a field guide to managing complexity and chaos in times of crisis for the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre. It moves through four phases. Assess: orient, decide whether this is a crisis, and if it is, act quickly on existing authority. Adapt: distribute decision-making, form small crews, build sensing networks. Exapt: repurpose existing assets, roles and processes into novel combinations. Transcend: stabilise, codify lessons before memory fades, and build resilience. Its advice on control is subtle: draconian constraints early, relaxation as the crisis is navigated, and planned re-imposition as things settle. Keep constraints that can be restored; avoid breaking those that cannot.

      The worst night in a receiver’s career usually begins with a phone call about an occupied building. Run the four phases on one.

      21 · Make the framework

      Three points: building Cynefin from the appointment itself

      Cynefin is a contextualisation framework, not a categorisation one. Its authors’ preferred way to use it is to build it from the material rather than drop the material into a ready-made picture. The Cynefin wiki’s primary method for this is three points. Groups take items from their situation and choose one exemplar for each of three extremes. On the right: a right solution exists that is self-evident or can be found by experts in time. Upper centre: with hindsight everyone would know what to do, but now there are multiple coherent ideas. Lower left: what we do matters little as long as we act decisively. The groups place every other item in tension with the exemplars and with each other. They then draw ribbons round the clear clusters, try to split whatever is left in the centre, and optionally divide the ordered area into Clear and Complicated. The debrief asks three questions: what is different, what is the same, and what surprises us.

      Run it here on the parade. Drag the fifteen numbered items, draw the boundaries, split order, then compare with the reference placement. In a lender workshop the differences between groups matter more than the answer.

      22 · Listening at scale

      Micro-narratives, the triad, and “more like these”

      A receiver arrives as a stranger to a place that already has its stories. The tenants have a version of what happened to the landlord; the agent has another; the lender’s relationship manager has a third, and each version is acting on the appointment before the receiver has read the file. Cynefin’s answer is not to replace those stories with a better one. Snowden has long been sceptical of “changing the narrative”: imposed stories tend to provoke anti-stories, the cynical retellings that amplify every gap between the promise and the experience. The alternative is to listen to the small stories people are already telling, at scale, and to let the people who tell them say what they mean.

      That is the idea behind SenseMaker®, which the Cynefin wiki describes as a tool for collecting self-interpreted micro-narratives: people are asked for a short story from their own experience, and then place that story themselves on a set of signifiers. The commonest signifier is the triad, a triangle with three balanced labels, none of them obviously right. The person puts a dot where their story sits between the three. A Cynefin Company post suggests that triads work partly because they sit within what a person can hold in mind at once: the story, and three forces acting on it. A dyad, a slider between two extremes that are both bad, catches a second judgement without inviting the socially correct answer.

      Two disciplines follow. The first is patterns first, stories second. Snowden’s warning is against expert interpretation: the consultant who finds and retells the “powerful story” has chosen the conclusion before reading the data. In SenseMaker work one looks at the stories that explain a statistical pattern, which protects the reader from being primed by the first anecdote that confirms what they hoped. The second is the standard question: what would we do to have more stories like these, and fewer like those? The wiki’s method of the same name has groups read story cards aloud, sort them into “more like this” and “less like that”, cluster the themes and turn them into actions. The question is plural on purpose. It asks for a shift in a pattern, not for a fix to one complaint.

      For a receiver the method does three things that no inspection report can. It finds the volatile actants of the estuarine map, because rumour shows up as a cluster long before it shows up as arrears. It gives tenants a voice that is theirs, not the agent’s summary of them. And it gives the lender evidence of change that is not a single figure: a cloud that moves. The instrument holds twenty-eight composite stories from one parade appointment, from residents, shop tenants, the lender, the agent and the neighbours, collected at month 1 and month 6. The triad asks what mattered most in each story: getting paid, being safe, or being heard. The dyad asks whether the receiver’s response was too slow or too hasty.

      Start with a pattern region, not with a story. Then read, and tag stories as more or fewer. The instrument turns your tags into micro-projects, one per theme. Finally, show the month-6 shift. Watch the lender’s centre in particular. On this parade it barely moves, which is the most useful thing the diagram says: a pattern that improves for the tenants can leave the person who appointed you unconvinced.

      25 · The first hundred days

      A Cynefin protocol for the first hundred days

      Everything in this treatise can be read as theory. This section is the opposite: thirteen moves, in order, that a receiver can carry into an appointment on the day the deed of appointment is signed. It is not a Cynefin Company method, and it should not be followed as a checklist, which would put a complex appointment into the Clear domain by decree. It is a direction of travel in the sense of the Vector Theory of Change: where to start, which instrument to reach for next, and what each move should have produced before the next one begins.

      The route is itself a Cynefin journey. Day 0 is chaotic by default, because the receiver does not yet know what is on fire; the first act is to secure the site and place insurance, then sense. Days 1 to 3 are spent deliberately in the aporetic centre, holding off the first diagnosis and building a contextualised framework with the people who know the place. The second and third weeks move into the complex: map the estuary, collect the stories, design the probes. Valuation and reporting are complicated work, done with experts on a stated basis. The routines that survive the probes are formalised along the blue line at around day 60, and protected against the catastrophic fold with weak-signal reporting at around day 75. On day 100 the receiver goes back to the centre and asks which issues have changed domain, because some will have.

      26 · The law across the domains

      The duties as governing constraints

      In Cynefin terms, laws, rules and codes are governing constraints: they give stability but are sensitive to change. The receiver’s legal position (powers from the deed and the Law of Property Act 1925, and the duties settled in Cuckmere Brick, Downsview, Medforth v Blake and Silven) frames every domain but replaces judgement in none.

      27 · Using Cynefin well

      Scaffolding, not categories

      Cynefin is easily misused as a four-box categorisation: put the problem in a box, apply the box’s recipe. Its own literature warns against that at every turn. The domains differ in kind, not value, and are drawn with organic curves rather than the straight lines of a matrix. The domain models are a cook’s handbook, not a recipe. The scaffolding image Snowden borrows from Ann Pendleton-Jullian and Alicia Juarrero makes the point: scaffolding supports a structure while it is being built and is then removed or absorbed. The aim is coherence, not alignment. Consistency comes from process and structure, not from a predefined product.

      For a receiver that means three habits. Decompose the appointment before choosing a method, because it is almost never in one domain. Watch the transitions more than the positions, because value is won and lost in the liminal states and at the inflection points. And use the lender’s language, not Cynefin’s: the framework is scaffolding for the receiver’s judgement, not a vocabulary to impose on a credit committee.

      Decompose

      • Which parts are Clear, Complicated, Complex, Chaotic?
      • Which part do we not yet understand?
      • Who owns each part?

      Watch the passages

      • Have we confirmed repeatability (alpha)?
      • Does the process still hold (beta)?
      • Is the routine resting on workarounds (delta)?

      Translate

      • Say “options and stop rules”, not “probes”.
      • Say “we do not yet know”, not “aporetic”.
      • Say “routine that needs a re-check”, not “Clear”.
